privacy

herder collects nothing you did not turn on.

herder is open-source software you run on your own machines. There is no herder account and no herder server: the apps and the terminal UI talk only to the daemons and the hub you run. Unless you turn on sharing diagnostics, nothing about how you use herder leaves your machines. This page says what does, and when.

Last updated 27 November 2026. It covers the herder daemon and CLI, the Mac and iPhone apps, and this website.

What stays with you

  • Sessions, transcripts, attachments and settings are kept by the herder daemon on the machine that runs them, and by the hub you link them to, if you run one. herder never sends them to us: we have nowhere to receive them.
  • Secrets live in your hub, and go only to the machines you grant them to.
  • Provider logins (Claude, Codex, Cursor, OpenCode and the rest) stay in each vendor's own CLI, under its config directory on your machine. herder never reads, stores, copies or relays them; it only runs the unmodified CLI.
  • Passkeys are created and kept by your device's keychain or your password manager. A daemon stores only each passkey's public key, and checks sign-ins against it on the machine itself.

The one third party: published artifacts

An agent can publish an artifact of its work, such as a screenshot, recording, page or log, to show it in its thread. Unless the session's project marks its artifacts private, the daemon then uploads the file to krowk.com for a public link that opens anywhere.

  • When: only when an agent publishes an artifact, never in the background.
  • What is sent: the file itself, its file name, its media type, its size and its SHA-256 checksum. Nothing else from the session goes with it, and no herder or provider account.
  • Who can see it: anyone who has the link. Links are made without a krowk account and expire after a day.
  • Private projects: with private artifacts on, nothing is uploaded. The artifact is kept only with the session, on your daemon, and shown in the apps from there.

krowk receives your machine's IP address with the upload, as any web server does.

Other connections herder makes

  • Updates: the daemon fetches herder releases from GitHub, and asks the npm registry for the latest version of the vendor CLIs it runs. These requests carry no data of yours.
  • Integrations you turn on: if you connect a Slack or Telegram bot, or add MCP servers, herder talks to those services on your behalf, under their terms.
  • The coding agents themselves talk to their vendors (Anthropic, OpenAI, Cursor and others) under those vendors' own privacy policies, exactly as they do without herder.

Sharing diagnostics: off unless you turn it on

To find out why herder is slow or crashes, you can choose to share diagnostics with us. It is off until you turn it on, and turning it off stops it at once:

  • Mac and iPhone apps: the apps ask once, on first launch, and the switch stays in Settings, under Diagnostics. It covers that app only.
  • Daemons and hubs: [telemetry] enabled = true in daemon.toml, or t on a machine in the terminal UI's machines panel (owners only). It covers that machine only.

While it is on, herder sends two kinds of data, both to services hosted in the EU:

  • Sentry (traces, errors and crashes): how long herder's own operations take, such as connecting by a kind of route (local network, Tailscale, tunnel, hub, WireGuard), the handshake, replaying a session, applying a kind of command, starting a turn and relaying through a hub; a sample of normal operations and every slow one. Errors and crashes, with the type of error and a stack trace of herder's code. In the apps also hangs, slow starts and slow screens (by the type names of their views), and, on the iPhone, sampled profiles of which functions the app spends its time in.
  • PostHog (anonymous measurements): how long connecting took and by which kind of route, why a connection was lost, its round-trip times and missed pings, how long each kind of command (such as sending a prompt) took to be answered and whether it was, how long a session took to replay and how many events it had, and how long a turn took to start. Every event and field is listed in the diagnostics docs.

Each comes with only: a random install id made for this purpose, the herder version (and, from a daemon, the Rust version it was built with), the operating system and its version, the device model (such as "iPhone17,1") and processor architecture, and whether it is an app or a daemon.

Never sent: prompts, transcripts, agent output, file names or paths, repository, branch, project or session names, machine names, hostnames, IP addresses, account names, email addresses, keys, tokens, passwords or secrets. herder removes anything that looks like one of these before sending, in the apps and in the daemon. The services see the IP address a report comes from, as any web server does; both are set to discard it.

If you share analytics with app developers in your Apple device's settings, Apple may also pass us anonymous crash reports for the apps; that is Apple's setting, and off unless you turn it on.

This website is hosted on GitHub Pages. The homepage uses PostHog, hosted in the EU, to count visits; it stores an anonymous identifier in your browser. Your theme choice is kept in your browser's local storage.
